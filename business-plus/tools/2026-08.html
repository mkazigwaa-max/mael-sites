A Billion of Your Customers Just Changed How They Search

The story of August isn’t a new gadget. It’s that the people who buy from you started asking AI instead of Google, and the tools you already pay for quietly got smarter and cheaper.

The Big One

Your next customer may never see a search results page

On July 31, OpenAI said ChatGPT had passed one billion weekly active users. It got there in under four years. Add the people using Gemini, Claude, and Copilot, and a meaningful slice of your market now asks an assistant, not a search engine, “who should I call about my roof?”

Here’s the part that matters: those assistants don’t browse the way people do. They read your website as text, and they recommend businesses whose sites plainly answer the basics. What you do. Where you work. What it roughly costs. How to book. If those answers live in a photo, a slogan, or nowhere, the assistant recommends someone else, and you never even know you were skipped.

Ten years ago the advice was “get on Google.” The 2026 version is simpler and cheaper: make your website answer a stranger’s five basic questions in plain written English. That’s most of the work of being visible to AI, and most businesses around you haven’t done it.

Mini Briefs

The engines got cheaper. Your tools get better.

OpenAI cut the price of one of its workhorse models by 80 percent on July 30, then cut its frontier model by more than 20 percent on August 21, that second one for three months. You may never buy from OpenAI, but the software you already use does. When the engine gets this much cheaper, AI features stop being a premium add-on and start showing up in the plan you already pay for. Watch your own tools’ update emails this fall, and if a vendor quoted you for “adding AI” in the spring, ask again.

Your books are learning to talk

Intuit has a plain-English chat in beta for QuickBooks Online, called Intuit Intelligence. Instead of digging through reports, you ask: “Who hasn’t paid me this month? What did I spend on subcontractors last quarter?” It shows its work so you can check before you act. Two things to know before you get excited: it’s admin users only, and each account gets a limited number of questions per month. If you’re on QuickBooks Online, it’s worth turning the beta on and spending those questions on the three things you always dig for.

Google is putting an assistant in your inbox’s house

On August 26, Google Workspace started rolling out Ask Gemini in Chat. It can search your company’s own Gmail, Drive, and Calendar and answer in plain English: “find the proposal we sent the Hendersons, and what did we quote?” If your team already runs on Google, it’s included in most paid Workspace plans rather than sold separately, though at launch it only works if your account language is set to English. Ten minutes of trying it will tell you if it sticks.

Tool of the Month

Google Business Profile

The free listing that puts you on Google Maps and in the panel beside a Google search. It matters more than it used to. When somebody asks an assistant who to call locally, it leans on the same public sources a person would: your own website, review sites, and your Business Profile. Those get read against each other, and a business whose details disagree is an easy one to skip.

Cost: $0.

The 15-minute Win

Ask an AI what it knows about your business.

  1. Open ChatGPT or Claude and type: “I’m considering hiring [your business name] in [your town]. What do they do, what do they charge, and how do I book them?”
  2. Read what comes back as if you were a stranger. Wrong services listed? No idea how to book? Confused you with a competitor?
  3. Every gap it shows you is a sentence missing from your website. Write the missing sentences this week, in plain English, on your homepage.

This is the same audit an agency would sell you. The tools give it away.

Late-breaking

The labels are coming. As of August 2, Anthropic embeds an invisible marker in text its newer Claude models generate, to meet Europe’s AI law, and it applies worldwide rather than only in Europe. Washington spent early August working on voluntary safety-testing rules with the major labs. The practical rule for you hasn’t changed: nothing goes out under your name that you haven’t read and made yours.
